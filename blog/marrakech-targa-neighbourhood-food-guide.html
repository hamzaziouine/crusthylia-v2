Targa, Marrakech — Neighbourhood Food Guide for Visitors

By the Crusthy'lia team · 27 March 2026 · 6 min read

Most visitors to Marrakech follow a well-worn path: the medina, Djemaa el-Fna, Majorelle Garden, a few riads, maybe a day trip to the Atlas. It's a great itinerary. But it misses something important about the city — the version of Marrakech that 900,000 people actually live in, outside the historic walls.

Targa is one of those places. A residential neighbourhood in the northwest of Marrakech, it's where families move when they want space, schools, and a quieter life without leaving the city. It's not on the tourist map. It doesn't have a hashtag. And precisely because of that, it offers something rare in Marrakech: a glimpse of everyday life in a modern Moroccan city, experienced at the pace of the people who live there.

Getting to Targa

Targa sits in the northwest of Marrakech, roughly 10–15 minutes by car from the centre of Guéliz (Marrakech's modern business district) and about 20 minutes from the edge of the medina depending on traffic. It's accessible by taxi — just say "Targa, Nakhil 2" and any driver will know the area. Ride-hail apps (InDrive, Careem) also serve the neighbourhood.

There's no compelling reason to walk from the medina to Targa — the distance is too far for a casual stroll. But coming out by taxi is easy and cheap by any international standard. A typical ride from Djemaa el-Fna will cost you 25–40 MAD (roughly €2.50–4).

Crusthy'lia: Your Food Anchor in Targa

If there's one place that anchors the food experience in Targa, it's Crusthy'lia at 890 Lotissement Nakhil 2. It's an artisan bakery, café, and restaurant that's become the neighbourhood's essential address — the place locals bring family visiting from Casablanca, the spot where the morning coffee has become a ritual, the restaurant that's reliably good when you don't want to think about where to eat.

For visitors, it's particularly valuable because it covers every meal of the day:

The food at Crusthy'lia isn't packaged for tourist appeal. It's just good food, made every day for the people who live there.

Morning (from 07:00): The bakery is in full swing. Fresh croissants, homemade pain au chocolat, brioches, and eleven breakfast formulas ranging from a quick espresso-and-croissant Express (MAD 35) to the full Royal spread (MAD 120). The Moroccan Beldi formula (MAD 55) — msemen, harcha, honey, butter and mint tea — is the authentic local morning experience done with professional quality.

Weekend brunch (Sat–Sun 09:00–14:00): Three curated brunch formulas — Parisian, Norwegian, American — attract families and visitors from across Marrakech. Book a day ahead via WhatsApp to secure your table. The Norwegian brunch with house-cured smoked salmon is genuinely remarkable at MAD 155.

Lunch and dinner (12:00–23:00): Burgers, pizzas, Moroccan tagines, pastas, salads — a full restaurant menu with homemade everything. The tagine de poulet citron-olives is a staple. The burgers use house-minced meat and bakery-baked buns.

Coffee and drinks all day: Proper espresso, flat white, matcha latte (real ceremonial grade matcha), cold brew, fresh-pressed juices. The café is a working space as much as a social one — WiFi, comfortable seating, power outlets.

What to Do Around Targa

Walk the residential streets. Targa is not a walking destination in the tourist sense, but a quiet stroll through the Nakhil residential areas gives you a clear picture of how middle-class Moroccan families actually live — the architecture, the daily rhythms, the parks and playgrounds, the neighbourhood mosques. It's the Marrakech you won't see on Instagram.

Visit a local market. The Targa area has neighbourhood markets where fresh produce, spices, herbs, and household goods are sold by vendors who don't adjust prices for foreigners. It's a genuinely local experience. Ask at Crusthy'lia for the nearest market — the team knows the neighbourhood.

Observe the school morning. Coming out to Targa on a weekday morning gives you a glimpse of the school run — parents dropping kids off, bakeries full of families grabbing breakfast, the neighbourhood at its most genuinely local. It's not a "tourist sight" but it's authentic Marrakech.

Why Targa is Worth the Trip for Food-Focused Visitors

There's a type of traveller for whom the best part of visiting a new city isn't the museums or the monuments — it's eating like a local in a neighbourhood where nobody's performing for tourists. Targa is that neighbourhood in Marrakech.

The food at Crusthy'lia — Marrakech Targa food at its best — isn't packaged for tourist appeal. The menu isn't full of items with "traditional Moroccan" in the name to signal authenticity. It's just good food, made every day for the people who live there. Tagines the way families eat them. Croissants baked because the neighbourhood needed a proper bakery. Coffee served because the barista actually cares.

Coming to Targa for a meal at Crusthy'lia, wandering the neighbourhood for an hour, and getting back into a taxi with real mint tea on your breath — that's a Marrakech memory that most visitors miss entirely.

Practical Notes for Visiting Targa

  • Language: French and Darija (Moroccan Arabic) are the main languages. English is understood at Crusthy'lia, but less commonly in the surrounding neighbourhood.
  • Dress: Targa is a residential neighbourhood. Modest dress (shoulders covered, no very short shorts) is respectful and appropriate.
  • Timing: The neighbourhood is most active in the early morning (school run) and early evening. Midday in summer can be very hot — best to be inside or in shade.
  • Transport back: Taxis are abundant in Targa and can be hailed on the street or via app.
